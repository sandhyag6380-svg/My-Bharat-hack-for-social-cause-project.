# Plant-Based Rapid Water Treatment for Metal Removal
PROBLEM STATEMENT
1. Background and Environmental Challenge
The accumulation of untreated industrial and municipal wastewater contaminated with toxic heavy metals poses a serious threat to public health and ecological balance. Metals can persist in aquatic environments and, depending on their concentration and chemical form, may harm aquatic organisms and create risks for people exposed through contaminated water or the food chain. Inadequately treated wastewater can carry these pollutants into rivers, lakes, groundwater, and coastal environments. Effective, accessible treatment methods are therefore needed to reduce metal contamination before wastewater is discharged or considered for reuse.
At the same time, agricultural and agro-industrial activities generate substantial quantities of plant and crop residues. When these materials are poorly managed through open burning, uncontrolled dumping, or unsuitable landfilling, they may contribute to air pollution, greenhouse-gas emissions, soil degradation, and the release of organic matter into surrounding environments. Plant biomass is often treated as a disposal problem even though it may contain useful compounds that could be recovered for value-added applications.
2. The Interconnected Problem
These challenges represent two linked environmental pressures: persistent metal contamination in water and the environmental burden associated with unmanaged agricultural biomass. They are often addressed separately, which can miss opportunities to connect waste management with pollution control. A sustainable approach should therefore explore whether plant residues can be converted into useful materials for water treatment, reducing waste while supporting remediation.
3. Limitations of Conventional Treatment
Conventional wastewater-treatment approaches may depend on chemical reagents, energy-intensive processes, specialized equipment, and substantial capital investment and also produce secondary pollution. Their suitability and cost can vary with the type and concentration of contaminants, the volume of wastewater, and the treatment setting. In parallel, biomass disposal practices may fail to recover the potential value of plant residues. These limitations highlight the need to investigate treatment technologies that are efficient, practical, and consistent with resource recovery and circular-economy principles.
 
4. Proposed Sustainable Approach
This project proposes a closed-loop valorization pathway in which discarded plant biomass is used as a resource for preparing plant-derived material for metal remediation. Aqueous plant extracts contain naturally occurring bioactive compounds that may assist material formation, surface functionalization, or stabilization. The resulting plant based materials are investigated for their ability to interact with and remove heavy metals from contaminated water. The approach connects biomass valorization with water treatment and aims to reduce reliance on avoidable waste while exploring a potentially affordable remediation option.
5. Prototype and Current Progress
A working prototype has been developed and demonstrated at our institute. The prototype provides a practical platform for demonstrating the treatment process and evaluating its performance under defined experimental conditions. The project aims to assess treatment speed and removal efficiency. The reported treatment time or removal percentage based on the pH, contact time, and flow.
Problem Statement Summary
The core problem is the simultaneous burden of heavy-metal-contaminated wastewater and unmanaged plant biomass. This project addresses both by investigating the use of discarded plant residues for water remediation, supported by a working prototype.
